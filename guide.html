Ten minutes, start to finish. Everything is on one screen.

What this is

A Bible reader built for someone who finds ordinary apps hard: large buttons, one screen, nothing to set up. It reads aloud, it listens, and it answers questions about what you are reading. Nothing you read, say or ask leaves the device.

The screen

The page is the Bible. Along the top is the toolbar: the chapter with arrows either side, then Speak, Search, Ask (and Margin, on the Study profile), text size (Aa) and Settings (⚙). Every button is big on purpose. If one is hard to hit, that is something we want to hear.

Reading

Scroll to read. Tap ◀ or ▶ to change chapter. Tap the chapter name — Genesis 6 — to pick any book and chapter from a grid.

Tap a verse to select it. Tap it again to unselect. Press and hold a verse to copy it.

Aa makes the text bigger or smaller, in steps. It stacks on top of the text size the device is already set to.

Read aloud

Tap Speak and the page is read to you, each word highlighted as it is spoken. The button becomes Pause while it reads; tap it to pause, and Speak again to continue. Tap a different verse while it is reading to jump there.

If you have selected a verse, Speak starts from it. With Ask open and a result selected, Speak reads that result instead of the page.

Rare Bible names are said the way the concordance spells them out — Habakkuk, Zerubbabel. Common names stay as you would expect.

Going somewhere

Three ways; use whichever is natural.

  • Tap the chapter name and pick from the grid.
  • Say it: tap Ask, tap the microphone, and say “Romans 8” or “John chapter 3 verse 16” or just “chapter 4”.
  • Ask Siri: “Open Romans 8 in Bible Reader.”

Search

Tap Search and type or say a word. You see every verse that contains it. The count at the top — “281 verses, with related forms” — opens to show what else was matched (loved, loveth, lovingkindness). Strict, in the row of chips, turns that off: only the exact word. Tap a result to open it in the page.

Ask

This is the part that is different. Tap Ask and type or say a question the way you would ask a friend — “What does this passage mean?”, “Who was this written to?”, “What happens next?”, “What does ‘plans to prosper you’ mean?” — or a name, a word, a place: “Noah”, “rest”, “who was Melchizedek”, “Tell me more about Jeremiah 29”.

A question about this verse or this passage is about the verse you have tapped on the page (or the chapter, if none is tapped), so you never have to say “Jeremiah 29:11” yourself. A question about something else — “Noah” — is answered about Noah, not about the page you are on. The chip that best fits the question is chosen for you: Names for a name, Commentary for “what does this mean”, Topics for “what can I learn from this”.

You get the reference works’ own words: what a name means (Noah — rest; repose; consolation), the dictionary entry, the concordance, the topical index, the commentary on the passage. The chips along the top of the panel — All · Names · Dictionary · Strong’s · Topics · Commentary · Cross-refs — narrow it. Cross-refs follows the verse you have selected on the page rather than what you typed.

Tap a result to select it; its scripture references become links you can open where they stand, and Speak reads the entry. Press and hold to copy it.

Suggestions offers the questions worth asking about the passage you are on — “Tell me more about Jeremiah 29:11”, “What was happening when this was written?” — so you type nothing. History keeps what you have asked, searched and gone to, so you can do it again with one tap.

Siri can ask for you: “Ask Bible Reader” — Siri says “What would you like to ask?” — and your question goes to Ask as if you had said it there.

With Apple Intelligence

On a device that supports Apple Intelligence you also get a short written answer above the list — a few sentences that answer your question directly, headed with what it is about (About Jeremiah 29:11), and what to read next. Every sentence cites the entry it came from, and those entries are the rows underneath, so you can check it. It always says what it is: Ask’s summary, not the Bible’s own words. Each chip gets its own short read of its results.

On a device without Apple Intelligence the app cannot write an answer of its own. Ask a question about a passage there and you get a prepared summary of the chapter instead — a short paragraph written ahead of time from Matthew Henry’s commentary, headed Prepared summary · About John 3, with a list under it of which of Henry’s entries each number stands for; tap one to open where it begins. It says plainly that it is about the whole chapter, not an answer to your question. Ask about a name or a word and there is nothing prepared, so the card says so. Everything else works the same.

Margin

On the Study reading profile (Settings › Reading Profile) there is one more button, Margin. Tap a verse, then Margin, and what is known about that verse comes up with no question asked — what a reference Bible keeps in its margin: the people and places it names, its words in the Hebrew or Greek they were written in, the other verses that speak to it under the word of your verse each one hangs from, and Matthew Henry’s commentary on the passage. On an iPad or a Mac it sits beside the page when the page has room to spare at your text size, and over the page when it hasn’t; the button in its corner moves it the other way if you’d rather. On a phone it comes up over the page.

Everything in it opens over the page: tap a verse there and you see it, can hear it, and can close it and be exactly where you were. The Words are the verse’s own words as chips, in order; tap one and it lights up on the page while Strong’s entry for it — the original word, how it is said, what it means — comes up with whatever Easton’s, Smith’s and the ISBE say under the same word (the chips with a small book have a dictionary entry). Tap a cross-reference’s heading, or a name, and the words it hangs from light up on the page too. Go to inside that card is the one thing that moves the page — and when it does, the bookmark ribbon appears so you can get back.

Talking to it

The microphone works in both Search and Ask. Tap it, say what you want, and stop talking — it ends on its own after a pause. Tap it again to stop early.

If it keeps mishearing you, Settings › Read Aloud › Better speech recognition sends your speech to Apple to be turned into words instead of doing it on the device. It is off unless you turn it on.

On a Mac

The same app, with a menu bar. The Reading menu has everything on the toolbar, with keyboard shortcuts: ⌘R Speak or Pause, ⌘[ and ⌘] for the previous and next chapter, ⌘F Search, ⌘K Ask, ⌘M Margin, ⌘+ and ⌘− for text size, and ⌘, for Settings. An iPad with a keyboard has the same shortcuts.

Settings worth knowing

  • Reading Profile — Simple, Standard or Study: one tap sets which buttons the toolbar shows and how big they are. Simple is the page and Speak alone, with the largest buttons (and, on an iPhone, Books and the chapter arrows in the bottom row); Study adds the Margin. Every switch it sets stays a switch of its own, further down.
  • Read Aloud — the voice, and Slower / Faster. Hear this voice previews it. The first choice uses whatever voice the device’s own Accessibility settings have chosen; that is the only way to get a Siri voice.
  • Study References — which reference works Ask searches. All on by default. Under Commentary, five more works — Matthew Henry’s complete commentary, Adam Clarke, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, Barnes on the New Testament and Spurgeon’s Treasury of David — are a 157 MB download rather than part of the app: tap Download once, on Wi-Fi if you can, and they join the Margin and Ask like the rest; Remove frees the space. Barnes covers only the New Testament and the Treasury only the Psalms, and the rows say so.
  • Reading — verse numbers on or off; text size.
  • About › Sources & Licenses — where every text came from and the terms it is under, each exportable as a text file.

What to tell us

  • Anything you could not hit — a button, a chip, the panel’s grab handle.
  • Anything you could not read — too small, too faint, the wrong colour in Night.
  • What the microphone heard versus what you said. Settings › Diagnostics has a log.
  • A Siri phrase that did not work, with Siri’s exact words back.
  • A wrong answer in Ask — say which chip and what you asked.
  • Anything that felt like one step too many.

There is nothing to sign into, and nothing is collected.
