Frequently asked questions

Tap a question to open it. Each answer starts short; where it's easier to follow on the screen, it walks through an example you can try in the app.

Are other translations supported?

The page you read is the King James Version unless you choose another: the Berean Standard Bible or the World English Bible can be the page instead (how). In the Margin you can show the same verse in up to six more Bibles, and you can read two Bibles side by side with Parallel.

Every Bible in the app is either in the public domain or freely licensed by its publisher. That's what lets the app include them at no cost, read them aloud, and let you share a verse. Most popular modern translations, such as the NIV, ESV and NKJV, are under copyright and can only be used with their publishers' permission, so they aren't in the app.

Try it: “Feed my sheep”, John 21:16

  1. Open Settings › Bibles. The Berean is already checked. Tap Download for the other five, then check the ones you'd like to see, and drag them into the order you want (how).

  2. Go to John 21, tap verse 16 and open the Margin. Under Parallel Translations, each Bible you checked shows the same verse. Tap Speak on one to hear it, in a Spanish or Chinese voice where the verse is in that language.

Search and Ask still read the King James, whichever Bible is on the page. When another Bible is the page, the Margin's Strong's words and measures are found in its words instead. When Ask puts a verse in today's words, it uses the Berean.

The Nueva Biblia Viva is a paraphrase, like the Living Bible in English, so its card says so. It often joins two or three verses into one sentence; the card then shows the verses it covers, such as “vv. 11–12”.

Each Bible's notice and licence, and anything this app changed, are listed under What's in the app and in the app's Settings › Sources & Licenses.

Can I change the reader from the King James to another Bible?

Yes. In Settings › Bibles › On the Page, choose the Berean Standard Bible, or the World English Bible once it's downloaded. The King James stays the default, and you can go back to it any time.

The page can be one of these three English Bibles. The Spanish and Chinese Bibles can be shown beside it, in the Margin or side by side, but not as the page itself, because the Margin's Strong's words, measures and names, and Ask, all work in English.

Try it: the Berean on the page

  1. Open Settings › Bibles. Under On the Page, choose Berean Standard Bible. (The tip “New: choose your Bibles” on the Settings button takes you there with Show Me.)

  2. Close Settings. You're on the same chapter and the same verse, and your ribbon is where you left it; only the words have changed. With VoiceOver on, you'll hear “Now reading the Berean Standard Bible.”

  3. Open the Margin on John 21:16. The King James is now the first card under Parallel Translations, so it's still a glance away. Strong's Words reads Shepherd, the Berean's word, where the King James says Feed.

  4. Go to Matthew 18. The Berean leaves out verse 11, which isn't in the oldest manuscripts, so its row says so instead of the numbers jumping from 10 to 12.

  5. To go back, choose King James Version (1769) under On the Page.

Can I choose which Bibles appear in Parallel Translations in the Margin?

Yes. In Settings › Bibles › In the Margin, a check puts a Bible in the Margin, and you can drag the Bibles into any order. The top one is the first card under Parallel Translations.

The Berean Standard Bible comes with the app. The Download button in the same list adds five more, about 28 MB: the World English Bible, two Spanish and two Chinese Bibles.

Try it: put the Spanish first, John 21:16

  1. Open Settings › Bibles. Under In the Margin each Bible on your device has a row. Tap a row to check or uncheck it.

  2. Drag Reina-Valera 1909 by its handle (the three lines on the right) to the top of the list. With VoiceOver, swipe up or down on the row and choose Move Up.

  3. Go to John 21, tap verse 16 and open the Margin. Under Parallel Translations, the Spanish comes first, then the rest in your order. The word card's In this verse follows the same order.

  • When another Bible is on the page, the King James joins this list, at the top until you move it, so it's always a glance away. The page's own Bible is never in the Margin.
  • With two Bibles side by side, the second Bible is left out of the Margin while it's open: it's already on the screen.
  • A Bible you uncheck, or remove with its download, keeps its place in your order for when it comes back.

Can I read two Bibles side by side, not just one verse in the Margin?

Yes. Turn on Parallel and the whole chapter appears in a second Bible, beside the page on an iPad or a Mac and under it on an iPhone. The two stay on the same verse as you read. You can also change the Bible on the page itself.

Choose what to set up:

Try it: John 3 in two Bibles

  1. Open Settings › Toolbar and turn on Parallel. (The tip “New: two Bibles side by side” on the Settings button takes you straight there with Show Me.) A Parallel button joins the toolbar.

  2. Go to John 3 and tap Parallel. The chapter appears in the Berean Standard Bible beside the King James. On an iPhone it's under the page; drag the bar between them to give either one more room. On an iPad in portrait at a very large text size, it goes under the page too, so both keep a readable line.

  3. Scroll either Bible and the other follows, a verse at a time. Tap verse 16 in either one and it's selected in both.

  4. To change the second Bible, tap its name at the top of its column, Berean Standard Bible ⌄, and choose another. You can choose any Bible on your device except the one on the page, Spanish and Chinese included. The same choice is in Settings › Bibles › Side by Side.

  5. Tap Parallel again to close it.

Change the Bible on the page

  1. Open Settings › Bibles. Under On the Page, choose Berean Standard Bible, or the World English Bible once it's downloaded.

  2. Close Settings. Your place doesn't move; only the words change. With Parallel open, the second Bible is now beside the Berean, and the King James can be the second Bible.

More, with what changes and what stays with the King James: Can I change the reader from the King James to another Bible?

Good to know

  • The Margin is about the page, the left column (or the top one on an iPhone). Its words, names, cross-references and measures are found in the page's Bible, not in the second one.
  • Red letters appear only when the King James is the page. The other Bibles' sources don't mark Jesus' words.
  • Search and Ask still read the King James for now, whichever Bible is on the page.
  • On an iPad or a Mac, Search, Ask and the Margin open from the bottom of the page while the second Bible stays beside it. The Margin can't sit beside the page then, so its Beside button is dimmed.
  • On an iPhone, only one of these can be open at the bottom at a time: Parallel, Search, Ask or the Margin. Opening one closes the one that was open.
  • Where a modern Bible says a measure another way, the Margin keeps the King James's words and marks them “(King James)”.

Read aloud with two Bibles

  • Speak reads the Bible you touched last. The filled speaker beside a Bible's name shows which. Tap a verse, scroll, or tap the speaker in either column to choose.
  • Double-tap a verse in either column to read from there, in that Bible.
  • Once reading, the voice keeps its Bible. Scrolling the other column won't switch it, and Pause then Speak carries on in the same Bible.
  • The verse being read is lit in both columns, so you can follow along in either. The word being spoken is lit only in the Bible being read.
  • A Spanish or Chinese Bible can sit beside the page, but Speak reads the page while one is there.
  • Closing Parallel while it's being read stops the reading.

What are Strong's numbers?

A number for every Hebrew and Greek word behind the King James Bible. The same number always means the same original word, whatever English word the translators chose.

In 1890 James Strong published his Exhaustive Concordance. It lists every word of the King James Version and every verse it's in. At the back are two small dictionaries: one for the Hebrew (and a little Aramaic) of the Old Testament, numbered H1 to H8674, and one for the Greek of the New Testament, numbered G1 to G5624. Each entry gives the word as it was written, how to say it, where it comes from, what it means, and every way the King James translates it.

The numbers matter because English and the original don't line up one to one. The King James sometimes uses one English word for two different originals, and sometimes several English words for one. John 21:15–17 has both kinds:

The King James saysWhereThe Greek wordStrong'sWhat it means
lovest21:15, 16agapaoG25to love (in a social or moral sense)
lovest21:17phileoG5368to be a friend to, to have affection for
Feed21:15, 17boskoG1006to pasture, to let graze
Feed21:16poimainoG4165to tend as a shepherd

Reading the English alone, you'd never know Jesus changed words. With the numbers, you can see it, and you can find every other verse that uses the same word.

Strong's dictionaries are in the public domain. Which Greek or Hebrew word stands behind each English word in the app comes from STEP Bible's word data (Tyndale House, Cambridge). Both are credited under What's in the app.

How do I use Strong's numbers in the app?

Tap a word under Strong's Words in the Margin, or ask about an English word in Ask and tap one of its Strong's entries. Either way a card opens with the original word, what it means, Strong's entry, and the other verses and words that go with it.

The Margin and Ask are on the Reference and Ask – Tell Me More reading profiles. If you don't see a Margin or Ask button, choose one of those in Settings › Reading Profile.

Choose where to start:

Try it: “Feed my sheep”, John 21:16

The card from the Margin is about the word in that verse: it shows how each English Bible translates it there.

  1. Go to John 21 and tap verse 16 to select it. Then tap Margin.

  2. Find Strong's Words. Each chip is a word of the verse as the King James has it, in the verse's order. A small book on a chip means a Bible dictionary has an article on that word too. Tap Feed.

  1. The card names the word behind “Feed”: poimaino, Strong's G4165, to tend as a shepherd. Under In this verse it shows how each English Bible on your iPad translates it here. The King James says Feed, and the Berean Standard Bible says Shepherd, which is closer to the Greek.

  2. Now go back to verses 15 and 17 and tap Feed there. It's a different word, bosko (G1006), to pasture. The King James says “Feed” all three times. The Greek says feed, shepherd, feed.

  3. Further down the card:

    • Strong's Concordance: the whole entry, with its verse references as links.
    • Other verses with this word: every verse where the Greek has poimaino, the King James word in bold. Tap one to read it on top of the card.
    • Other Greek words translated “feed”: bosko, trepho, chortazo and more, each with its number. Tap one to open its own card. Done brings you back.

  4. To keep what you found, select a word or a phrase on the card and copy it, or tap Share to send the whole card with its source to Notes, Messages or Mail.

Try it: every word for “feed”

The card from Ask is about the word itself, not one verse: it lists every verse it's in, and every other Hebrew and Greek word the King James also translates “feed”.

  1. Tap Ask and type feed (or tap the microphone and say it).

  2. Under the answer, tap the Strong's chip to see only Strong's entries. Each row is one Hebrew or Greek word that the King James translates “feed”, with the other ways it translates that word. Tap feed, rule (poimaino).

  1. The card is poimaino, Strong's G4165, to tend as a shepherd. There's no In this verse here, because you didn't come from a verse. Instead, Other verses with this word lists all ten verses with poimaino, John 21:16 among them.

  2. Other Greek words translated “feed” lists the rest of the family, each with its Strong's number, what it means and how many verses it's in: bosko (G1006) to pasture, chortazo (G5526), trepho (G5142) and more. Below them, the Hebrew words, led by ra`ah (H7462), the shepherd's word of Psalm 23. Tap any of them to open its own card on top. Done brings you back.

  3. Numbers in Strong's entry, like 4166, are links too: tap one to open that word's card.

On either card, select a word or a phrase to copy it, or tap Share to send the whole card with its source to Notes, Messages or Mail.

What do the Bible's old measurements and times mean?

When a verse names a measure, a coin or a time, the Margin's Measures & Time section says what it is today, in US measures with metric beside them. Tap one for its own card.

It covers weights (a shekel, a talent), lengths (a cubit, a furlong, a day's journey), dry and liquid measures, money (a penny, a mite), the watches of the night, the hours of the day, and the months. For each example below, select the verse, open the Margin, and look for Measures & Time.

Choose an example:

A distance: John 6:19

“So when they had rowed about five and twenty or thirty furlongs, they see Jesus walking on the sea.”

  1. Go to John 6, tap verse 19, and open the Margin. Under Measures & Time, thirty furlongs comes to about 3.4 miles (5.6 kilometers). The Berean Standard Bible says “about three or four miles”.

  2. Tap thirty furlongs for the furlong's own card: one furlong on its own, something to picture, how it relates to a Roman mile, every verse with furlongs in it, and the Bible dictionaries' articles.

A time of night: Luke 12:38

“And if he shall come in the second watch, or come in the third watch, and find them so, blessed are those servants.”

  1. Go to Luke 12, tap verse 38, and open the Margin. Measures & Time has both watches, each on a band of the night: the second watch, about 9 in the evening to midnight, and the third, about midnight to 3 in the morning. The servants are waiting up through the dead of night.

  2. Tap the third watch. Its card says what a watch was and why it was called one, how many there were, every verse with that watch, and the dictionaries on the night watches.

Hours of the day: Matthew 27:45

“Now from the sixth hour there was darkness over all the land unto the ninth hour.”

  1. Go to Matthew 27, tap verse 45, and open the Margin. The day was counted from sunrise, about 6 in the morning, so the sixth hour is about noon and the ninth hour about 3 in the afternoon. Both are on a band of the day. The darkness lasted from midday to mid-afternoon.

  2. Tap the ninth hour. Its card says how hours were counted, notes that this was the hour of prayer and of the evening sacrifice, and lists the nine verses with the ninth hour.

A weight of gold: Genesis 24:22

“The man took a golden earring of half a shekel weight, and two bracelets for her hands of ten shekels weight of gold.”

  1. Go to Genesis 24, tap verse 22, and open the Margin. Measures & Time has both gifts. The earring, half a shekel (a bekah), is about ⅕ ounce (5.7 grams), the weight of a US quarter. The bracelets, ten shekels, are about 4 ounces (114 grams).

  2. Each one says what the gold would be worth today, as of the month the app was built, and sets it beside a price the Bible itself gives. Achan risked everything for fifty shekels of gold (Joshua 7:21). A tap on that line opens the verse.

  3. Tap ten shekels for the shekel's card: one shekel on its own, how it relates to the gerah and the bekah, every verse with shekels (98), and the dictionaries' articles. What was this worth? asks Ask to tell the whole story.

All measures and times, at the foot of the section, opens the whole table: every weight, length, measure, coin, time of day and month, each with its own card.

You can ask too. In Ask, try “What was a shekel worth?” or “What time was the ninth hour?”. The answer is written from the same worked-out figures, and any number the app didn't give is left out.

Sizes are approximate. Scholars differ, and the old dictionaries give the history. Today's value of gold and silver is as of the month the app was built.
