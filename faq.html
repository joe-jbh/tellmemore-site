What are Strong's numbers?

A number for every Hebrew and Greek word behind the King James Bible. The same number always means the same original word, whatever English word the translators chose.

In 1890 James Strong published his Exhaustive Concordance. It lists every word of the King James Version and every verse it's in. At the back are two small dictionaries: one for the Hebrew (and a little Aramaic) of the Old Testament, numbered H1 to H8674, and one for the Greek of the New Testament, numbered G1 to G5624. Each entry gives the word as it was written, how to say it, where it comes from, what it means, and every way the King James translates it.

The numbers matter because English and the original don't line up one to one. The King James sometimes uses one English word for two different originals, and sometimes several English words for one. John 21:15–17 has both kinds:

The King James saysWhereThe Greek wordStrong'sWhat it means
lovest21:15, 16agapaoG25to love (in a social or moral sense)
lovest21:17phileoG5368to be a friend to, to have affection for
Feed21:15, 17boskoG1006to pasture, to let graze
Feed21:16poimainoG4165to tend as a shepherd

Reading the English alone, you'd never know Jesus changed words. With the numbers, you can see it, and you can find every other verse that uses the same word.

Strong's dictionaries are in the public domain. Which Greek or Hebrew word stands behind each English word in the app comes from STEP Bible's word data (Tyndale House, Cambridge). Both are credited under What's in the app.

How do I use Strong's numbers in the app?Example: “Feed” in John 21:16

Open the Margin beside a verse and tap a word under Strong's Words. A card opens with the original word, what it means, Strong's entry, and every other verse that uses it.

The Margin is on the Reference and Ask – Tell Me More reading profiles. If you don't see a Margin button, choose one of those in Settings › Reading Profile.

Try it: “Feed my sheep”, John 21:16

  1. Go to John 21 and tap verse 16 to select it. Then tap Margin.

  2. Find Strong's Words. Each chip is a word of the verse as the King James has it, in the verse's order. A small book on a chip means a Bible dictionary has an article on that word too. Tap Feed.

  1. The card names the word behind “Feed”: poimaino, Strong's G4165, to tend as a shepherd. Under In this verse it shows how each English Bible on your iPad translates it here. The King James says Feed, and the Berean Standard Bible says Shepherd, which is closer to the Greek.

  2. Now go back to verses 15 and 17 and tap Feed there. It's a different word, bosko (G1006), to pasture. The King James says “Feed” all three times. The Greek says feed, shepherd, feed.

  3. Further down the card:

    • Strong's Concordance: the whole entry, with its verse references as links.
    • Other verses with this word: every verse where the Greek has poimaino, the King James word in bold. Tap one to read it on top of the card.
    • Other Greek words translated “feed”: bosko, trepho, chortazo and more, each with its number. Tap one to open its own card. Done brings you back.

  4. To keep what you found, select a word or a phrase on the card and copy it, or tap Share to send the whole card with its source to Notes, Messages or Mail.

The same works in Ask: when an answer or a result is a Strong's entry, tapping it opens the same card.

What do the Bible's old measurements and times mean?Example: furlongs and the fourth watch

When a verse names a measure, a coin or a time, the Margin's Measures & Time section says what it is today, in US measures with metric beside them. Tap one for its own card.

It covers weights (a shekel, a talent), lengths (a cubit, a furlong, a day's journey), dry and liquid measures, money (a penny, a mite), the watches of the night, the hours of the day, and the months. Where a verse gives a price in gold or silver, it also says what the metal would be worth today, and what the same amount bought in Bible times.

Try it: the night Jesus walked on the sea

John and Mark tell the same night two ways: John says how far the disciples had rowed, and Mark says what time it was.

  1. Go to John 6, tap verse 19, and open the Margin. “They had rowed about five and twenty or thirty furlongs.” Under Measures & Time, the furlongs come to about 3.4 miles (5.6 kilometers). The Berean Standard Bible says “about three or four miles”.

  1. Tap thirty furlongs. The furlong's card gives one furlong on its own, something to picture, how it relates to a Roman mile, every verse with furlongs in it, and the Bible dictionaries' articles, each a tap away.

  2. Now go to Mark 6:48: “about the fourth watch of the night he cometh unto them, walking upon the sea.” Measures & Time says the fourth watch was about 3 to 6 in the morning, and a band of the night shows it as the last stretch before dawn.

  1. Tap the watch for its card: what a watch was and why it was called one, how many there were, every verse with that watch, and the dictionaries on the night watches. The hours work the same way. The day was counted from sunrise, so “the ninth hour” is about 3 in the afternoon.

  2. All measures and times, at the foot of the section, opens the whole table: every weight, length, measure, coin, time of day and month, each with its own card.

You can ask too. In Ask, try “What was a shekel worth?” or “What time was the ninth hour?”. The answer is written from the same worked-out figures, and any number the app didn't give is left out.

Sizes are approximate. Scholars differ, and the old dictionaries give the history. Today's value of gold and silver is as of the month the app was built.
