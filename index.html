Built for hands that shake and eyes that tire.

Get the app Read the guide

Most Bible apps are built for people who find iPads easy. This one was built for someone who doesn't.

It started as one app for one reader: a mother with Parkinson's and failing vision, who loved her Bible and couldn't use any of the apps that put it on a screen. The buttons were too small. The menus went three deep. The text was fine at arm's length and gone up close. Every app assumed a steady hand and a patient eye, and she had neither.

So this one assumes nothing.

One screen

Big buttons, nothing to set up

The page is the Bible. Above it, a few buttons, each larger than any you've pressed on an iPad, each labelled in words. No menus, no gestures to learn, nothing to confirm.

Speak

It reads to you

Tap Speak and the page is read aloud, every word lit as it's spoken. Pause and resume with one tap. Tap a verse and it picks up from there. It knows how to say Nebuchadnezzar.

Listen

It listens to you

Every place you can type, you can talk instead. Say "Romans 8" and you're there. Say "who was Melchizedek" and you get the answer. Speech is recognised on the iPad itself.

Ask

It answers you

Ask about a name, a word, a passage, and you get the reference works' own words — what the name means, the dictionary, Strong's, Nave's, Matthew Henry on the very verse you're reading. On an iPad with Apple Intelligence, a short written answer to your question, every sentence cited.

Margin

A study Bible's margin

On the Study profile, what is known about a verse sits beside the page: the people it names, its words in the Hebrew or Greek, the verses that speak to it, and commentary — five more commentaries, one download away.

Siri

Ask Siri

"Open John 3:16 in Bible Reader." From the lock screen, from Shortcuts, from across the room. "Ask Bible Reader" and your question goes straight to Ask.

Everything stays on your iPad

No account. No sign-in. No subscription. Nothing you read or say leaves the device — not because of a privacy policy, but because there is no code that could send it. The King James text and every reference work are public domain, credited on their own page inside the app, and any of them can be exported as a plain text file.

Free

It was written as a gift, for one person. It's free for everyone else for the same reason.

Requires an iPad running iPadOS 26 or later, or a Mac with Apple silicon running macOS 26. Written answers require Apple Intelligence.
